Every game developer starts with the simplest tool available. For me, it was a single file named `todo.txt`. 

I dumped everything in there. Feature ideas sat next to critical bug fixes. Random YouTube links to synthwave tracks I wanted to use for boss fights were buried under lists of UI tweaks. The workflow was incredibly basic. When I finished a task, I just typed `--DONE` next to it. 

It felt highly productive at first. You open a file, write down a thought, and close it. The friction is zero.

## Losing Track

The illusion of progress broke down as the game grew. The file became a massive, unreadable scroll of text. 

I could no longer tell what was important. I lost track of dependencies. A task like "fix player jump" might rely on a physics overhaul buried three hundred lines further down the page. I spent more time reading through old `--DONE` entries trying to find my place than actually writing code. 

The flat structure of a text file simply cannot handle the multidimensional nature of game development. I needed a system that understood priority, effort, and categories.

![A screenshot of a chaotic text file filled with tasks and links](/images/placeholders/messy-todo-file.png)

## Codecks to the Rescue

I moved my entire workflow over to Codecks. This platform replaces standard kanban boards with a trading card metaphor designed specifically for game development. The architectural shift instantly changed how I approached the project.

I dismantled my monolithic text document piece by piece. I extracted every single task, rogue idea, and reference link, binding each one to an individual card. I then dealt these cards into specific decks based on their core function. I built a design deck to hold ambient audio tracks, concept art, and mechanical inspiration. I created a dedicated defect deck to strictly isolate game-breaking bugs from routine feature additions. This physical separation of concerns proved crucial. Mixing bugs with new features in a single list inevitably leads to prioritizing fun mechanics over necessary stability.

The visual hierarchy immediately cleared my cognitive load. Codecks allows you to draw specific tasks from your broader decks into a personal hand for the current milestone. I no longer had to parse hundreds of lines of chaotic text just to figure out my morning agenda. I could simply open my dashboard, look at the five active cards sitting in my hand, and immediately start writing C#. The overwhelming noise of the broader project stayed entirely out of sight until I explicitly chose to look at it.

## Assigning Weights

The fundamental flaw of a plain text file is the total lack of temporal context. A task is either pending or complete. This binary state creates a massive psychological trap for developers. You might spend six grueling days writing a custom physics controller and only get to type `--DONE` once. The very next day, you fix three minor typos in a menu and cross off three distinct items. A flat document falsely tells you Tuesday was significantly more productive than your entire previous week.

Moving to a dedicated project tool introduced the concept of weight. I started assigning concrete effort values to every single card before bringing it into my active workspace. A complex database migration gets a high effort score. Swapping a placeholder texture gets a low one. 

This simple requirement forces a moment of honest reflection before any actual programming begins. You have to interrogate the card. You must decide if you actually understand the scope of the problem.

## Productivity

Humans are notoriously terrible at estimating software timelines. We naturally assume the best-case scenario where no unforeseen bugs emerge. A robust tracking system acts as a harsh mirror against that optimism. 

When I planned a new combat mechanic, my gut told me it would take two days. Instead of blindly committing to that timeframe, I looked back at my archived decks. The historical data proved that similar combat tweaks consistently took a full week to balance and polish. I stopped guessing. I adjusted my project roadmap immediately to reflect reality.

![A burnup chart demonstrating steady development velocity across multiple project milestones](/images/placeholders/velocity-burnup-chart.png)

This data-driven approach completely changed my relationship with the project. Unrealistic timelines inevitably lead to self-imposed crunch. You end up working late into the night trying to meet a completely arbitrary deadline born from an uneducated guess. 

Tracking an accurate, realistic development velocity established hard boundaries around my time. It revealed exactly how much capacity I actually had in a given week. I stopped fighting my own schedule. The project kept moving forward steadily, and I stopped burning myself out to maintain an impossible pace.

## Making it Look Cool

Visuals matter for organization. A wall of text is fatiguing to read, even when it is broken up into cards. 

I use icons from game-icons.net to categorize different decks and card types. Giving the UI deck a distinct gear icon and the audio deck a waveform symbol makes scanning the board effortless. It gives the project workspace a cohesive, game-ready feel. 

![A screenshot of a clean Codecks dashboard featuring custom icons](/images/placeholders/codecks-dashboard.png)

Managing a game project is hard enough without fighting your own notes. Moving away from a raw text file forced me to treat my development process with the same care I give to my codebase.
